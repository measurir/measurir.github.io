# Calibration and Metrological Traceability: What Engineers Need to Know

Calibration helps establish the relationship between an instrument's indications and reference values, including the associated measurement uncertainty. It is a foundation of trustworthy testing, but it is not a substitute for correct operation or maintenance.

## Calibration versus adjustment and verification

These terms have distinct meanings:

- **Calibration** establishes a relationship between indications and reference values under specified conditions.
- **Adjustment** changes the measuring system so that its indications meet desired criteria.
- **Verification** provides objective evidence that specified requirements are fulfilled.
- **Maintenance** preserves or restores functional condition; it does not, by itself, demonstrate metrological performance.

An instrument can pass a verification check without a full calibration, but the scope and acceptance criteria must be defined.

## What traceability means

Metrological traceability is a property of a measurement result whereby it can be related to a reference through a documented, unbroken chain of calibrations, each contributing to measurement uncertainty. Traceability is a property of the result, not merely a sticker on the instrument.

A certificate should identify the device, measurement points, results, uncertainty, method, environmental conditions where relevant, and the reference standards used. Review whether the calibration range and uncertainty are suitable for the intended decision.

## Choose calibration intervals based on risk

There is no single interval that suits every instrument. Consider:

- Frequency and intensity of use.
- Transport, vibration, shock, and environmental exposure.
- Stability history and previous calibration results.
- Consequences of an undetected bias.
- Manufacturer recommendations and contractual or regulatory requirements.
- Intermediate checks and control-chart evidence.

A high-consequence load-measuring system may need more frequent checks than a low-risk indicator used only for rough monitoring.

## An effective instrument register

Maintain a register with a unique asset ID, manufacturer and model, serial number, location, range, resolution, calibration due date, status, and owner. Link certificates and maintenance records to the same ID. Clearly identify instruments that are overdue, damaged, restricted, or out of service.

## What to do after an out-of-tolerance result

1. Quarantine the instrument or restrict its use.
2. Establish the period since the last acceptable evidence.
3. Identify measurements and decisions potentially affected.
4. Assess the likely magnitude and direction of error where possible.
5. Repeat or independently verify critical tests if justified.
6. Document corrective action, customer notification where required, and authorization to return the device to service.

Do not automatically invalidate every historical result; assess the technical evidence and the risk of each affected decision.

## Field checks between calibrations

A check with a reference artifact can reveal drift, damage, or setup problems. Define the check method, frequency, limits, records, and action on failure. A field check is not equivalent to calibration unless it actually establishes the relevant calibration relationship.

## Conclusion

Calibration and traceability work best within a managed system: appropriate instruments, defined intervals, competent personnel, controlled records, and a process for handling failures. The objective is not paperwork alone; it is confidence that measurements are fit for their intended use.
